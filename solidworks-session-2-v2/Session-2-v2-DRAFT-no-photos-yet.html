1. Read the code: what "Ø10 H7" means

Reading about 10 minutes. Practice 5 minutes.

Why this part matters

No machine makes a part at exactly 10.000 mm. Every real part comes out a little bigger or a little smaller. So on the drawing you must say how far from 10 the part may go. That allowed range is the tolerance (tolerância).

For most sizes, a simple range is enough. But where a part must fit into another part, the range must be exact. Engineers write that range with a short code, like Ø10 H7. This part teaches you to read it.

Piece 1: the nominal size

The nominal size (medida nominal) is the round number you draw in the model. Here it is 10 mm. The symbol Ø means diameter. The letter and the number that come after it say how far the real part may be from 10.

First, how small are these numbers?

Fit tolerances are very small. We measure them in µm, called micrometre or mícron. One µm is 0.001 mm. So 15 µm is 0.015 mm.

Look at Figure 1.2. A whole H7 band at Ø10 is 15 µm. That is about one fifth of a human hair. Your shop can hold this, but only with the right tools. You will see those tools below.

Piece 2: the letter says where the band sits

Think of the allowed range as a band (campo de tolerância). The letter says where this band sits, compared to the nominal size.

  • Capital letter = a hole (furo). Small letter = a shaft (eixo). "Shaft" means any round outside surface: a pin, a shaft, the outside of a bushing.
  • H (a hole): the band starts exactly at 10.000 and goes up. An H hole is never smaller than the nominal size.
  • f, g (a shaft): the band sits below 10.000. The shaft is always a little smaller.
  • h (a shaft): the band ends exactly at 10.000 and goes down.
  • k, m, n, p, s (a shaft): the band sits above 10.000. The shaft is a little bigger. Letters later in the alphabet are bigger.
  • js: the band sits half above and half below. At Ø10, js5 is 10.000 ±0.003 mm.

The distance from the nominal size to the edge of the band is called the deviation (afastamento). For Ø10 H7 the deviations are +0.015 and 0.

Piece 3: the number says how wide the band is

The number is the IT grade (qualidade IT, or grau de tolerância). A smaller number is a narrower band. A narrower band is harder to make, so it costs more. The band also gets wider on bigger parts, because big parts are harder to hold exactly.

Size rangeIT6IT7IT8IT9IT11
6 to 10 mm9 µm15 µm22 µm36 µm90 µm
10 to 18 mm11 µm18 µm27 µm43 µm110 µm
18 to 30 mm13 µm21 µm33 µm52 µm130 µm
30 to 50 mm16 µm25 µm39 µm62 µm160 µm

Put the pieces together: H starts at 10.000, and IT7 at 10 mm is 15 µm wide. So Ø10 H7 = 10.000 to 10.015 mm.

Why the hole gets the H

A hole is finished with a tool of one fixed size, like a reamer (alargador). You cannot adjust a reamer to a new size. A shaft is turned on a lathe (torno), so the machinist can simply take off a little more metal.

So the normal way is: always make the hole H, and pick the shaft letter to get the fit you want. This is called the hole basis system (sistema furo-base). It keeps the shop on standard tools.

Watch out: H7 is not free A drill alone holds about IT11 to IT13. At Ø10 that is 90 µm or more, about 6 to 20 times wider than H7. If you write H7, the shop must drill and then ream, bore or grind the hole. That is a real extra step and real extra money. Write H7 only where a part must fit.
Worked example: the bore of a conveyor pulley

The part. A drive pulley (polia) for a small conveyor. It sits on a Ø20 shaft with a key. The pulley must sit centred on the shaft, or the belt runs unevenly.

Step 1. What you write

On the pulley drawing you write Ø20 H7 on the bore.

Step 2. Read the code

  • Ø20 = nominal size 20 mm.
  • H = the band starts at 20.000 and goes up.
  • 7 = IT7. The size 20 is in the "18 to 30 mm" row, so IT7 = 21 µm = 0.021 mm.

Step 3. The two limits

Smallest bore 20.000 mm. Biggest bore 20.021 mm. Anything in between is a good part.

Step 4. What the shop does

The turner bores the hole on the lathe, or drills and reams it. Then they check it with a bore gauge or a plug gauge.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You write only "Ø20". The shop may just drill it. The bore can come out at 20.10 mm. Now the pulley sits 0.1 mm loose on the shaft. It wobbles, the key takes all the load, the key seat wears, and the belt runs to one side.

Key idea Ø10 H7 = size 10, band starts at 10.000 (H), band is 15 µm wide (7). So the hole is 10.000 to 10.015 mm. Capital letter for holes, small letter for shafts.

2. The three families of fits

Reading about 12 minutes. Practice 5 minutes.

What a fit is

A fit (ajuste) is what happens when one part goes inside another part. A shaft in a hole. A pin in a plate. A bearing on a shaft.

You never choose a fit by one number. You choose two bands: the hole band and the shaft band. What matters is how the two bands sit next to each other. There are only three possible results. We call them the three families.

Key idea First ask what the parts must do: slide, sit exactly, or never move. That gives you the family. Only after that do you pick the exact code.

Family 1: clearance fit, the parts always slide

ajuste com folga

The shaft is always smaller than the hole. Even the biggest shaft is smaller than the smallest hole. So there is always a small gap, called clearance (folga).

What you feel: the parts go together by hand. They can turn or slide.

Where you use it: a shaft that turns in a bushing, a guide rod that slides, a pin you pull out by hand, a hinge.

Family 2: transition fit, "maybe a gap, maybe tight"

ajuste incerto

The two bands overlap a little. So it depends on which parts you get:

  • A big hole with a small shaft: a tiny gap.
  • A small hole with a big shaft: a tiny press.

You do not know which case you get until you measure. That is why Brazilian shops call it incerto, "uncertain".

What you feel: the parts go together with a light tap of a plastic hammer (martelo de nylon), or a small press. They come apart with a light pull or a puller.

Where you use it: a part that must sit exactly in place but must still come off for service. A gear or pulley hub on a shaft, a dowel pin in a plate.

Family 3: interference fit, the parts are pressed

ajuste com interferência, or ajuste prensado

The shaft is always bigger than the hole. The extra metal is called the interference (interferência). When you push the shaft in, both parts squeeze. That squeeze grips the shaft very hard.

How the shop assembles it: with a hydraulic press (prensa). For bigger interference, they heat the outer part so it grows, or cool the shaft so it shrinks.

Where you use it: a part that must never move and never come apart. A bushing pressed into a frame, a pin that must stay, a gear pressed on a shaft for good.

The price: you cannot get it apart without a puller (saca-polia) or a press, and often you damage it.

Three questions to pick the family
  1. Must it slide or turn? Clearance.
  2. Must it sit exactly, and still come apart for service? Transition.
  3. Must it never move and never come apart? Interference.

The short list you can trust

ISO 286 has hundreds of possible codes. You only need the ten preferred fits. Use them and the shop stays on normal tools and normal gauges. The hole is always H, so only the shaft letter changes.

Worked example: an index pin in a welding fixture

The part. A welding fixture has a Ø10 index pin (pino de indexação). The operator pushes the pin into a hole to hold a swing plate in position, then pulls it out by hand to release it. This happens hundreds of times a day.

Step 1. Pick the family

The pin must go in and out by hand, so it must slide: clearance. But it must also locate well, with almost no play.

Step 2. Pick the fit

From the short list: slides and still locates = H7/g6.

Step 3. The limits

  • Hole Ø10 H7 = 10.000 to 10.015 mm
  • Pin Ø10 g6 = 9.986 to 9.995 mm

Step 4. Check the gap

  • Smallest gap = smallest hole minus biggest pin = 10.000 − 9.995 = 0.005 mm (5 µm)
  • Biggest gap = biggest hole minus smallest pin = 10.015 − 9.986 = 0.029 mm (29 µm)

There is always a gap, so it always slides. The gap is at most 0.029 mm, so the swing plate sits within a few hundredths.

What goes wrong with the other families

  • With p6 (interference), the pin is 10.015 to 10.024. The operator cannot push it in by hand at all.
  • With no fit, the shop drills the hole to maybe 10.10. The pin rattles with 0.1 mm of play. Your weld parts come out 0.1 mm off every time.

3. The SolidWorks tool that finds the numbers for you

Reading about 8 minutes. Practice 12 minutes.

Key idea SolidWorks has the ISO 286 tables inside it. You pick the fit code. SolidWorks calculates the numbers and writes them on the dimension. You never open a table book.

Step by step: an H7 hole

  1. Click the diameter dimension of the hole. This works in a part and in a drawing.
  2. The PropertyManager opens on the left. This is the panel with the settings of the thing you clicked.
  3. Open the Tolerance/Precision box.
  4. Set Tolerance Type to Fit with tolerance.
  5. Set Classification to Clearance, Transitional or Press. This shortens the two lists to fits of that family.
  6. Open the Hole Fit list and pick H7.
  7. Set the precision to 3 decimals, so you see 0.015 and not 0.02.
  8. Click the green check.

For a shaft, do the same, but use the Shaft Fit list, for example g6. On a single part you fill only one list.

What the dimension shows

With Fit with tolerance, the dimension shows the code and the two deviations, for example Ø10 H7 (+0.015 / +0.000). The deviations are how far each limit is from the nominal size. To get the real limits, add them to the nominal size: 10 + 0.015 = 10.015, and 10 + 0.000 = 10.000.

If SolidWorks shows 0 for a fit, the size is outside the range the standard covers for that code. Pick another code.

Your new habit Old habit: no tolerance on holes where parts fit, and the same ±0.1 on everything. New rule: every hole that touches a bearing, a pin, a bushing or a shaft gets a fit from this tool. The general note in Part 7 covers every other size. Nothing gets a hand typed ±0.1 any more.

The Hole Wizard is the SolidWorks tool that makes standard holes. Since SolidWorks 2019 it can also carry the tolerance. Then the drawing callout fills itself in.

4. Bearings: the two seats you design

Reading about 15 minutes. Practice 12 minutes.

What a bearing does

A rolling bearing (rolamento) lets a shaft turn with very little friction, and it carries the load of the shaft. Inside, small steel balls roll between two rings.

  • The inner ring (anel interno) sits on the shaft.
  • The outer ring (anel externo) sits in the housing.
  • The balls (esferas) roll between them. A cage (gaiola) keeps the balls apart.
  • Many bearings also have seals or shields (vedações) on the sides to keep grease in and dirt out.

The most common type is the deep groove ball bearing (rolamento rígido de esferas). Its number starts with 6, like 6205.

How to read a bearing number

Take 6205. The "62" is the series, a light size family. The "05" is the bore code. For bore codes 04 and up, multiply by 5: 05 x 5 = 25 mm bore.

A 6205 is 25 mm bore, 52 mm outside, 15 mm wide.

Key idea A bearing is a bought part. Its bore and its outside are already made. You do not choose them. You only choose two things: the shaft seat (assento do eixo) and the housing bore (furo do mancal).

The bearing comes a little under size

Bearing makers follow ISO 492. For a normal 6205, the bore comes 24.990 to 25.000 mm. It is never above 25.000. The outside is also a hair under 52.000.

That is why bearing seats do not use the normal tables in the obvious way. A shaft marked "k5" gives a light press on a bearing, even though k is "transition" for a normal hole. The bearing bore is already a little small, so the fit comes out tighter.

The big rule: which ring gets the tight fit?

The ring that turns against the load direction needs the tight fit. The other ring can have a normal, easier fit.

Why? Picture a belt that pulls the shaft down. The load always points down. The inner ring turns with the shaft. So every point of the inner ring passes under the load, again and again, every turn. If the inner ring is loose on the shaft, it slowly rolls around the shaft, like a loose ring on your finger. This is called creep. Creep polishes and wears the shaft seat. The shop will find a shiny, worn seat and a bearing that makes noise.

The outer ring does not turn. The load always presses on the same spot. So it does not creep, and it can have a normal fit.

Case 1: the shaft turns (the normal case)

This is almost every conveyor roller, drive shaft and gearbox shaft. The load comes from one direction and stays there, like belt pull or weight.

Worked example: the bearing block of a conveyor drive shaft

The part. A conveyor drive shaft turns in two 6205 bearings. Each bearing sits in a machined steel block, the housing (mancal). The belt pulls the shaft down.

Step 1. Which ring turns against the load?

The shaft turns and the belt load always points the same way. So the inner ring turns against the load. That is Case 1.

Step 2. Pick the classes

The shaft is 25 mm, in the "17 to 100 mm" row, with a normal load. Shaft seat = k5. Housing bore = H7.

Step 3. The limits

  • Shaft seat Ø25 k5 = 25.002 to 25.011 mm
  • Bearing bore, from the factory = 24.990 to 25.000 mm
  • Housing bore Ø52 H7 = 52.000 to 52.030 mm

Step 4. Check the fit on the shaft

  • Smallest press = smallest shaft minus biggest bore = 25.002 − 25.000 = 2 µm
  • Biggest press = biggest shaft minus smallest bore = 25.011 − 24.990 = 21 µm

Always a light press. The inner ring cannot creep. In the housing, the outer ring has a small clearance, so it slides in and the bearing is not squeezed.

Step 5. The shoulders

From the bearing catalogue: shaft shoulder at least da min = 30 mm. Housing shoulder at most Da max = 47 mm. Corner radius 1.0 mm max. More on this below.

What goes wrong on the shop floor

  • Shaft too loose (for example h6, or just "Ø25"): the inner ring creeps. After some weeks the seat is worn and shiny, the bearing rattles, and the shaft must be replaced.
  • Housing too tight (for example a press fit): the outer ring is squeezed smaller. The balls lose their small internal gap. The bearing runs hot and fails early.
  • Both tight: you cannot mount it without heat, and it may be damaged when you try.

The shoulder: what the bearing pushes against

A shoulder (encosto) is a step on the shaft or in the housing. The bearing rests against it, so it cannot slide along the shaft.

  • The shaft shoulder must be big enough to touch the inner ring well. The catalogue gives the smallest size as da min. For 6205, da min = 30 mm.
  • The housing shoulder must reach far enough inward to hold the outer ring well. So its inside diameter can be at most Da max. For 6205, Da max = 47 mm.
  • The inside corner of the shoulder needs care. The bearing ring has a rounded corner. Your shaft corner must be smaller than it, so the ring sits flat against the shoulder. For 6205 the shaft corner radius is 1.0 mm max.
  • Instead of a radius, you can cut a small groove in the corner, called an undercut (rebaixo, or canal de alívio). It also lets the grinding wheel finish the seat right up to the shoulder.

Surface finish on the seat

Ra (rugosidade média) is the average roughness of a surface in µm. A smaller number is a smoother surface. Ask for Ra 0.8 on a bearing seat, and Ra 0.4 on a press fit seat. Also ask for the seat to be round and straight within about half of its size tolerance. Session 3 shows how to write that with GD&T.

Free models with real dimensions Do not draw bearings by hand. SKF, Schaeffler, NSK, igus and Misumi give free CAD models, in native SolidWorks format, after you make an account. The product page also gives you da min, Da max and the corner radius.

5. Dowel pins: the cheap way to put parts back in the same place

Reading about 10 minutes. Practice 10 minutes.

What a dowel pin does

A dowel pin (pino de guia, or pino cilíndrico) is a short, exact steel cylinder. You put two of them between two parts, like a fixture plate and a base.

Screws hold the parts together, but screws do not locate them. A screw goes through a clearance hole. For an M8 screw that hole is about Ø9, so the part can shift up to half a millimetre.

Two dowel pins in exact holes fix the position. When you take the fixture off for service and put it back, it goes to the same place, within hundredths of a millimetre.

Which pin to buy

  • ISO 8734: hardened and ground, tolerance m6. Use these for fixtures and machine parts.
  • ISO 2338: plain steel, not hardened, also m6. Cheaper. Good enough for covers and brackets.
  • ISO 8735: hardened, with a thread inside one end. Use it in blind holes, and to pull the pin out later with a screw.

You do not make pins. You buy them. Your job is the hole.

The hole: H7, reamed

Give the pin hole H7. With an m6 pin this is a transition fit: sometimes a tiny gap, sometimes a tiny press. The pin goes in with a light press and stays.

An H7 hole cannot be made with a drill alone. Tell the shop how: drill 0.2 to 0.5 mm under size, then ream to H7.

Two pins, far apart, one of them free in one direction

One pin stops the part from sliding, but the part can still turn around it. So you use two pins. Put them as far apart as you can. The further apart, the less the part can twist.

But two round, exact holes in both parts can jam. If the distance between the holes is off by only 0.02 mm in one part, the pins fight each other. There are two standard fixes:

  1. One round hole, and one slot (rasgo) in the second place. The slot points toward the first pin. The pin can move along the slot, but not across it.
  2. Two round holes, and a diamond pin (pino losango, or pino com rebaixo) in the second hole. This pin has two flats ground on it, so it touches the hole on two narrow bands only.
Worked example: a fixture plate on a machine base

The part. A 400 mm long fixture plate is screwed to a machine base with four M8 screws. It must go back in the same place after every tool change. You add two Ø8 pins, 300 mm apart.

Step 1. Pick the pin

A machine fixture, so hardened pins: ISO 8734, Ø8 m6. The pin is 8.006 to 8.015 mm.

Step 2. The holes

Ø8 H7 = 8.000 to 8.015 mm, reamed. In the plate: one round hole and one 8 H7 slot. In the base: two round holes.

Step 3. Check the fit

  • Biggest hole minus smallest pin = 8.015 − 8.006 = 9 µm gap
  • Smallest hole minus biggest pin = 8.000 − 8.015 = −15 µm, so 15 µm press

From 9 µm gap to 15 µm press: a transition fit, as planned.

Step 4. Length and depth

Engagement 1 to 1.5 x 8 = 8 to 12 mm in each part. The plate is 12 mm thick, so a 20 mm pin gives 10 mm in each part.

Step 5. The note for the shop

DRILL Ø7.8, REAM Ø8 H7. REAM THE PLATE AND BASE CLAMPED TOGETHER. Reaming both parts together makes the hole positions match perfectly.

What goes wrong on the shop floor

  • Only drilled holes: the pins are loose. The plate shifts 0.1 mm or more each time you put it back.
  • Two round holes in both parts, drilled separately: the pins jam, and the maintenance team hammers the plate on. Soon the holes are damaged.
  • Pins too close together: the plate can still twist a little around them.
Blind holes A blind hole (furo cego) does not go all the way through. A pin in a blind hole traps air and oil, so it will not go down. And you cannot push it out from below. Use an ISO 8735 pin with an internal thread. Or make the hole a through hole when you can.

In SolidWorks

Hole Wizard has a hole type made for pins, since SolidWorks 2013.

Hole Wizard > Type tab > hole type: Dowel Hole, then pick the pin size

Set the tolerance to H7 in the same panel, so the hole callout shows it. The pins are in the Design Library, under Toolbox, in the ISO pins folder.

  3. Add two ISO 8734 pins from Toolbox and mate them.
  4. On the drawing, add the note: "Drill Ø7.8, ream Ø8 H7 with plates clamped together".

6. Bushings, sliding shafts and parts you take apart

Reading about 14 minutes. Practice 10 minutes.

Three jobs in this part

  1. A shaft that turns slowly in a bronze bushing.
  2. A shaft that slides in a linear ball bushing.
  3. A part that must come apart for service, like a pulley.

Each job needs a different fit. We go through them one at a time.

Job 1: the bronze bushing, a simple bearing

A bushing (bucha) is a tube. A bronze bushing (bucha de bronze) is a tube of bronze that works as a simple bearing. The shaft turns inside it, with grease or oil. There are no balls.

Use it for slow or small movements: a pivot arm, a hinge, a swing clamp, a lever. It is cheap, small, and it takes shock well.

The bushing is pressed into its housing, so it never turns in the housing. The shaft turns inside the bushing.

The fits for a bronze bushing

  • Housing bore: H7. The bushing's outside comes a little bigger from the maker, so it presses in.
  • Shaft: f7, for a normal running clearance. Use e7 if it runs fast or hot, because it needs more gap.
The trap: the bore closes in When you press a bushing into a housing, the housing squeezes it. The outside gets smaller, and so does the inside. If you plan the shaft clearance with the "as bought" bore, your shaft may not fit after pressing, or it may seize when it warms up.
Fix: use the maker's after-press bore size, or ream the bore after pressing.
Worked example 1: the pivot of a swing arm

The part. A swing arm (braço basculante) on a packing machine turns about 90° and back, slowly. The pivot is a Ø20 shaft in a pressed bronze bushing.

Step 1. Pick the fits

Housing bore for the bushing: H7, at the bushing's outside size from the catalogue. Shaft: slow and cool, so f7.

Step 2. The shaft limits

Ø20 f7 = 19.959 to 19.980 mm.

Step 3. The bore after pressing

Look in the catalogue for the bore after pressing. Say the maker gives 20.000 to 20.033 mm after pressing into an H7 housing. That is an H8 band.

Step 4. Check the clearance

  • Smallest gap = 20.000 − 19.980 = 20 µm
  • Biggest gap = 20.033 − 19.959 = 74 µm

Always a running gap, big enough for grease. This is the H8/f7 "close running" fit from Part 2.

What goes wrong on the shop floor

You use the "as bought" bore and give the shaft h6. After pressing, the bore has closed in. The fitter cannot push the shaft in, and has to sand or ream the bushing by hand. Or it goes in tight and seizes (engripa) after an hour of running.

Job 2: sliding shafts and linear ball bushings

For a plain sliding shaft, two fits cover almost everything:

  • H7/g6: it slides and still locates well. Guide rods, sliding pins.
  • H8/f7: it runs. More clearance, less friction, less accuracy.

For smooth, long movement you use a linear ball bushing (bucha linear, or rolamento linear). It is a bushing with rows of balls inside. The balls roll on the shaft and go round in a loop. It needs a hardened, ground shaft (eixo temperado e retificado), which you usually buy ready made.

  • Shaft: g6 for normal clearance.
  • Housing bore for the bushing: H7.
  • Linear bushings come with their own numbers. Check the maker page.
Worked example 2: a guide shaft in a linear bushing

The part. A pick and place axis has a carriage on two Ø20 guide shafts. Each side of the carriage holds an LM20UU linear bushing, which is 20 mm inside and 32 mm outside.

Step 1. The shaft

Buy a hardened, ground shaft in g6. Ø20 g6 = 19.980 to 19.993 mm.

Step 2. The carriage bore for the bushing

Ø32 H7. The size 32 is in the "30 to 50 mm" row, IT7 = 25 µm. So 32.000 to 32.025 mm.

Step 3. Check with the maker

Open the maker's page for the bushing. If it asks for other values, use theirs.

What goes wrong on the shop floor

  • Carriage bore too tight: it squeezes the bushing. The balls lose their gap, the carriage feels stiff and rough.
  • A soft, turned shaft instead of a hardened one: the balls dig grooves into it within weeks.

Job 3: parts that must come apart for service

  • H7/h6: goes together by hand, comes apart by hand, almost no play. Use it for covers, spacers and centring rings.
  • H7/k6: a light tap to fit, a light pull or a puller to remove. Use it for a gear or a pulley hub that must sit true but still come off.
  • Never use p6, s6 or u6 on something you open often. You will not get it apart without damage.
Worked example 3: a pulley you change with the belt

The part. A timing belt pulley on a Ø20 motor side shaft, with a key (chaveta) for the torque. Maintenance changes the pulley once a year.

Step 1. Pick the fit

It must run true, so no play. It must come off, so no press fit. That is H7/k6.

Step 2. The limits

  • Pulley bore Ø20 H7 = 20.000 to 20.021 mm
  • Shaft Ø20 k6 = 20.002 to 20.015 mm

Step 3. Check the fit

  • Biggest gap = 20.021 − 20.002 = 19 µm
  • Biggest press = 20.015 − 20.000 = 15 µm

A transition fit. The key carries the torque. The fit only centres the pulley.

What goes wrong on the shop floor

  • With p6: maintenance needs a puller and heat, and bends the pulley flange.
  • With a loose drilled bore: the pulley wobbles, the belt wears on one side, and the key seat hammers out.

  2. Model a Ø20 pivot shaft. Give it f7 with the Fit tool.
  3. Model a pulley hub with a Ø20 bore, H7, and a Ø20 shaft end, k6.
  4. Read the numbers SolidWorks gives, and compare them with worked examples 1 and 3.

7. Stop writing ±0.1 on everything

Reading about 8 minutes. Practice 5 minutes.

Key idea One note near the title block covers every size that has no tolerance of its own. Then you write a tolerance only where the part really needs one. This fixes two of your habits at once, and it makes your parts cheaper.

What the note says

Put this note on the drawing, near the title block (legenda):

UNLESS OTHERWISE STATED: GENERAL TOLERANCES ISO 2768-m

"Unless otherwise stated" means: every size with no tolerance written next to it gets the tolerance from ISO 2768, class m. Class m (medium) is the normal choice for machined parts. Class f (fine) is tighter. Class c (coarse) is looser.

SizeClass fClass mClass c
0.5 to 6 mm±0.05±0.1±0.2 to 0.3
6 to 30 mm±0.1±0.2±0.5
30 to 120 mm±0.15±0.3±0.8
120 to 400 mm±0.2±0.5±1.2
400 to 1000 mm±0.3±0.8±2.0

So a 100 mm edge with no tolerance is ±0.3 mm. That is normal shop work, and it costs nothing extra. Your old ±0.1 on the same edge asked for finer work and more money, for no reason.

Edge breaks, in the same note

A sharp edge (canto vivo) cuts hands and gets damaged. The shop can break it with a small chamfer or file, but only if you ask. You said you do not add edge break notes. Add this line:

BREAK ALL SHARP EDGES 0.3 MAX (QUEBRAR CANTOS VIVOS)

For chamfers and radii that you do draw, ISO 2768 class m allows ±0.2 mm up to 3 mm, ±0.5 mm from 3 to 6 mm, and ±1.0 mm above 6 mm.

Worked example: a sheet metal cover

The part. A 2 mm steel cover (tampa de chapa), 250 x 180 mm, with four Ø6.6 holes for M6 screws and a 15 mm bent flange.

Step 1. Which sizes need a fit?

None. No bearing, no pin, no shaft touches this part. The screws go through clearance holes. So the general note covers every size.

Step 2. What the note gives each size

  • 250 mm length: 120 to 400 mm row, so ±0.5.
  • 180 mm width: same row, ±0.5.
  • 15 mm flange: 6 to 30 mm row, ±0.2.
  • Ø6.6 holes: 6 to 30 mm row, ±0.2. An M6 screw is 6 mm, so it still passes.

Step 3. Write nothing else

No hand typed tolerances. The drawing is clean, and the shop knows every limit.

What goes wrong on the shop floor

  • No note at all: the shop guesses, or calls you, or uses its own rule. You cannot reject a bad part, because you never said what "good" is.
  • ±0.1 on everything: bent sheet metal cannot hold ±0.1 on a flange at normal cost. The shop quotes more, or rejects the job.

Bends have their own tricks. Many sheet metal shops ask for class c on bent sizes. Ask yours. Session 5 covers sheet metal.

The small print on ISO 2768

ISO 2768-1 (sizes and angles) is still current. Brazil has it as ABNT NBR ISO 2768-1. ISO 22081:2021 took the place of ISO 2768-2, which covered form and position with the old classes H, K and L. Many shops still write "ISO 2768-mK", and everybody understands it. Write "ISO 2768-m" for sizes. When you later add form and position control, use ISO 22081 or normal GD&T. That is Session 3.

In SolidWorks: put it in the template once

Open your drawing template. Add the notes with:

Insert > Annotations > Note

Place them just above or next to the title block. Then save the template, so every new drawing starts with the notes.

Practice, 5 minutes in SolidWorks
  1. Open one of your drawing templates.
  2. Add the two notes near the title block. Save the template.
  3. Open an old drawing. Find every hand typed ±0.1. Delete the ones the general note now covers.

Worked example: one conveyor roller, start to finish

The part. A conveyor roller: a tube with two 6205 bearings, turning on a fixed Ø25 axle. The tube turns, the axle stays still.

  1. Bearings in the tube: the outer ring turns with the tube, and the load stays down. Row "outer ring turns": tube bore Ø52 K7.
  2. Bearings on the axle: the inner ring stands still. Axle seat Ø25 h6, or g6 if you want it to slide on easily.
  3. End caps that come off for service: H7/h6.
  4. Tube length, cap sizes, bolt holes: no fit, the ISO 2768-m note covers them.

Put each fit in with the SolidWorks Fit tool, and let SolidWorks write the numbers. Then check the bearing maker's page, because the roller is a Case 2 bearing.

Your three new habits
  1. Any hole that touches a bought part gets a fit class from the table above.
  2. Every drawing gets the ISO 2768-m note and the edge break note.
  3. No hand typed tolerances and no hand typed hole notes. Let the Fit tool, Hole Wizard and Hole Callout write them.

Where the numbers come from

  • ISO 286-1 and ISO 286-2 tolerance values.
  • ISO 2768-1 general tolerances. ISO 2768-2 replaced by ISO 22081:2021.
  • Bearing seats: short form of the SKF table "Seat tolerances for standard conditions". Bearing tolerances: ISO 492 normal class.
  • Dowel pins: ISO 2338, ISO 8734, ISO 8735.
  • SolidWorks: help.solidworks.com pages for Fit Tolerances, Hole Wizard tolerances (2019), Dowel Holes (2013) and Hole Callout.
