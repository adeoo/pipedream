📜 **Where we are in history.** The *Manifesto*, 1848 still. Railways are stitching continents together; telegraph wires hum; British gunboats have just forced open China's ports in the name of free trade. Capital is going global — and Marx, watching, writes what remains the most clear-eyed description of capitalism ever put on paper. Strangely, it reads at first like praise.

**The lesson.** Marxists do not say capitalism is merely evil. Marx insisted the bourgeoisie "has played a most revolutionary part" in history. It shattered feudalism, dissolved the divine right of kings, tore apart the "motley feudal ties" that bound peasant to lord — and replaced them all with one universal relation: money. It built forces of production greater "than have all preceding generations together." Where the old world was static, capitalism *cannot stand still*: it must constantly revolutionize its machines, its products, its markets, or die. "All that is solid melts into air, all that is sacred is profaned" — كل ما هو صلب يذوب في الهواء.

But hear the second half of the argument. This restless system is *compelled to expand*. "The need of a constantly expanding market chases the bourgeoisie over the entire surface of the globe. It must nestle everywhere, settle everywhere, establish connexions everywhere." That sentence, written in 1848, is a prophecy of everything the coming weeks of this course will examine: the colonization of the Arab world, the plantations and mines of Latin America, the "opening" of every economy that tried to develop on its own terms. When we reach Paraguay — a country that in the 1860s was building railways, foundries, and universal schooling *without* foreign debt, and was destroyed for it — remember this passage. The world market was not offered to the periphery; it was imposed, بالحديد والنار, *a sangre y fuego* — by iron and fire, by blood and fire.

So hold both truths at once, as Marx did: capitalism is history's most productive system *and* its most relentlessly destructive one — and the same dynamic drives both. That refusal of simple moralizing is what makes the analysis cut.

📖 **From the source.** The *Manifesto*:

> "The bourgeoisie... draws all, even the most barbarian, nations into civilisation. The cheap prices of its commodities are the heavy artillery with which it batters down all Chinese walls... It compels all nations, on pain of extinction, to adopt the bourgeois mode of production."

"On pain of extinction." Marx chose his words carefully.

🤔 **Reflection.**
1. Where do you see "all that is solid melting" today — in work, in cities, in family life, in Beirut or Asunción?
2. What in your two countries was destroyed not because it failed, but because it stood in the market's way?
