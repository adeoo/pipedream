📜 **Where we are in history.** London, the 1860s. Marx spends his days in the British Museum reading room, coughing, broke, burying children he cannot afford to keep alive, filling notebook after notebook. In 1867 the first volume of *Das Kapital* appears. He warns readers that the opening chapter is the hardest — because he begins not with revolution or class war, but with the humblest thing imaginable: a single object for sale.

**The lesson.** Last week gave you the framework. This week we go inside the machine and find where profit actually comes from — the argument Marx considered his real scientific achievement.

He begins with the **commodity** — سلعة, *mercancía* — because capitalism presents itself as "an immense accumulation of commodities." And every commodity, he says, has two faces.

Its **use-value** is what it does for you: bread feeds, a coat warms, khubz and a jacket meet real human needs. Use-value is concrete and qualitative — you cannot compare the usefulness of bread to the usefulness of a coat; they satisfy different wants.

Its **exchange-value** is what it trades for: this coat = twenty kilos of bread = a certain sum of money. And here is the puzzle that had baffled economists for a century. If bread and coats are qualitatively incomparable, *how can the market equate them at all?* When two things are set equal, something equal must exist in both. What?

Not their weight, not their color, not their usefulness. Strip away every physical property of the coat and the bread and only one thing remains common to them: both are products of **human labor**. That is Marx's answer, and everything else follows from it. The hidden substance that lets a market equate a coat with bread is congealed human effort — "human labor in the abstract," measured in time.

Notice what this move does. It makes the market — which appears to be a realm of things, prices, and supply curves — into a relation between *people*, between the hours of their lives. When you buy a shirt in Beirut or Asunción, you are, whether you know it or not, entering a relation with the women who sewed it. Capitalism's genius is that this relation appears to you as a price tag. Tomorrow we sharpen this into the measure of value itself.

📖 **From the source.** *Capital*, Volume I, the opening line:

> "The wealth of societies in which the capitalist mode of production prevails appears as an 'immense collection of commodities'; the individual commodity appears as its elementary form. Our investigation therefore begins with the analysis of the commodity."

Marx called the commodity the **cell-form** of capitalism — the way a biologist starts with a single cell to understand an organism.

🤔 **Reflection.**
1. Pick one object within arm's reach. Trace it: who made it, where, under what conditions? How much of that is visible in the object itself?
2. Something can have enormous use-value and no exchange-value — air, a mother's work at home, water from a spring. What does capitalism systematically fail to count?
