📜 **Where we are in history.** The longest chapter in *Capital* is not philosophy — it is documentary. Marx quarried the reports of British factory inspectors and recorded, name by name, what the ten-hour and twelve-hour days did to human bodies: Mary Anne Walkley, twenty years old, dead after twenty-six and a half hours of continuous work in a dressmaking shop, "died from long hours of work in an over-crowded workroom." He wanted the ledger of it in the record forever.

**The lesson.** Yesterday's arithmetic left one thing undetermined, and Marx makes it the hinge of the whole book: **how long is the working day?**

Economic theory cannot answer. The capitalist, as a buyer, has bought a day's labor-power and has every right to consume it fully — sixteen hours if he can. The worker, as a seller, has every right to insist that a "day" not consume the very capacity he must sell again tomorrow. Both appeal to the law of exchange. Both are correct.

Marx's conclusion is one of the great sentences in political economy: *"Between equal rights, force decides."* The length of the working day is not set by economics, morality, or the market. It is set by the balance of **class power** — decided in strikes, in legislation wrung from frightened parliaments, in blood.

This is why the eight-hour day is not a gift from enlightened employers or a natural product of growth. It was taken. Chicago, 1886 — Haymarket, the hanged organizers, and the reason much of the world marks the first of May while the country where it happened does not. It is why *el primero de mayo* and عيد العمال are not holidays about barbecues but commemorations of a war over hours.

And it is why those gains are never permanent. Look at what has quietly happened in your lifetime: the "flexible" contract, the app that makes you self-employed so no hour is ever officially overtime, the phone that reopens the workday at ten at night, the Lebanese teacher with three jobs because one no longer feeds a family. Marx's chapter is not history. Capital pushes on the working day always and everywhere, and only organized resistance pushes back.

📖 **From the source.** *Capital*, Volume I:

> "There is here therefore an antinomy, right against right, both equally bearing the seal of the law of exchange. Between equal rights, force decides. Hence, in the history of capitalist production, the establishment of a norm for the working day presents itself as a struggle over the limits of that day, a struggle between collective capital, i.e. the class of capitalists, and collective labour, i.e. the working class."

🤔 **Reflection.**
1. How many hours do you actually work — counting the commute, the messages after dinner, the invisible preparation? Who decided that number?
2. Name a labor protection in either of your countries that exists only because people fought for it. Is it stronger or weaker today than a decade ago?
