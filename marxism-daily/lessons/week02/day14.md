📜 **Where we are in history.** We close the week where *Capital* itself pauses for something stranger than economics. At the end of the first chapter, Marx breaks into the language of religion and magic to describe the marketplace — and produces the concept that would fascinate the next century of thinkers, from Lukács to Fanon's readers to every critic of consumer society since.

**The lesson.** **Commodity fetishism** — الطابع الصنمي للسلعة, *el fetichismo de la mercancía*.

Think about how economic reality reaches you. Prices "rise." Markets "get nervous." The currency "collapses." The dollar "decides" whether your family in Beirut eats meat this month. Coffee "is worth" this much today. Everything is phrased as though objects and numbers were the actors, moving by laws of their own like weather.

But every one of those movements is, at bottom, a relation between human beings — between the coffee grower and the trader, between the central banker and the depositor, between whole classes and nations. Marx's point is that capitalism does not merely *hide* these relations; it genuinely makes them *appear* as properties of things. "A definite social relation between men themselves assumes here, for them, the fantastic form of a relation between things."

He calls it fetishism deliberately, borrowing the anthropologists' term for an idol carved by human hands that its makers then kneel before as an independent power. We produce the market through our own labor and exchange, and then it stands over us as fate. Lebanese savers watched their life's work evaporate by decree of "the banking sector" — as though no one decided anything. The soy price "moves" and a Paraguayan farming family is off its land.

This closes the week's arc. Start with a commodity (Day 8), find labor time inside it (Day 9), find labor-power on the market (Day 10), find surplus value in the working day (Day 11), see the struggle over its length (Day 12), see the wage hide it (Day 13) — and now see why the whole arrangement presents itself as a natural order of things rather than a relation among people who could, in principle, arrange things otherwise.

That last clause is the entire wager of Marxism.

📖 **From the source.** *Capital*, Volume I:

> "It is nothing but the definite social relation between men themselves which assumes here, for them, the fantastic form of a relation between things. In order, therefore, to find an analogy we must take flight into the misty realm of religion."
