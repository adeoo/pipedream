Reading a Geometry Chart

Every frame ever designed can be written down as a small table of numbers. Today you learn to open that table — a real one, from a real touring bike — and read it like a sentence instead of a spreadsheet.

Warm-up: retrieve before you build

Two quick questions from Lesson 1, from memory — no peeking at the cheat sheet. Pulling a name out of your head a day later is exactly what locks it in for good.

What a geometry chart is

A geometry chart is the bike industry's way of writing a frame down on paper. It's a table: one column per frame size, one row per measurement. Lengths are in millimeters, angles in degrees. Nothing more mysterious than that — the intimidating part is only that there are a dozen rows and you don't yet know which ones matter.

Here's the good news for a future framebuilder: the chart isn't just a shopping aid. It is, quite literally, a compressed design. When you draw your own touring frame in the capstone, your finished design will be a geometry chart plus a full-scale drawing. Learning to read charts now is learning to read the documents you'll one day write.

A real one: the Surly Disc Trucker

The Surly Disc Trucker is one of the best-known steel touring bikes in the world — close cousin to the kind of frame you want to build. Below is a slice of Surly's official chart: two sizes, six rows. (The full chart has more of both; we're starting with the rows you can already name from Lesson 1.)

Measurement56cm58cm
Seat tube length560 mm580 mm
Effective top tube575 mm590 mm
Seat tube angle73°72.5°
Head tube angle72°72°
Chainstay length450 mm450 mm
Wheelbase1051 mm1060 mm

Two things to notice before we decode the rows:

  • The size name is a real measurement. "56cm" isn't a T-shirt size — it's the seat tube length, 560 mm. That's the classic way frames are sized.
  • Not everything grows with size. Going from 56 to 58, the tubes get longer — but the chainstays stay 450 mm and the head angle stays 72°. Some numbers serve the rider's body; others serve the bike's behavior. Separating those two jobs is the key insight of this whole week.

Where each number lives on the frame

The six rows, decoded

  • Seat tube length (560 mm) — bottom bracket up to the top of the seat tube. Job: fit. It's the "size" of the frame and sets how tall the frame stands under you.
  • Effective top tube (575 mm) — head tube to seat tube, measured level with the ground, not along the actual tube. Job: fit — it's the biggest factor in how stretched-out you are. Why "effective"? Many modern top tubes slope downward; measuring horizontally lets you compare a sloping frame with a classic level one (Sheldon Brown on frame sizing).
  • Seat tube angle (73°) — how far the seat tube leans back from horizontal. Job: fit — it places your hips relative to the pedals. Steeper = further forward.
  • Head tube angle (72°) — the lean of the head tube, which is the lean of your steering axis. Job: behavior — it's the single biggest personality number on the chart. Slacker (smaller number) = calmer steering. We give it its own lesson soon.
  • Chainstay length (450 mm) — bottom bracket to rear dropout. Job: behavior — long stays put the rear wheel further back: more stability, more heel room for panniers. Racing bikes run about 410 mm; the Trucker's 450 mm is the touring signature.
  • Wheelbase (1051 mm) — front axle to rear axle, the sum of all the other choices. Job: behavior — a long wheelbase is a long, steady ship. Over a meter, like here, is firmly touring territory.

The one-sentence skill: to read any chart, find your size column, then ask of each row — "does this number serve the rider's body, or the bike's behavior?" Fit numbers change with size; behavior numbers are the designer's opinion about how the bike should ride, and often stay the same across sizes.

Your real-world assignment

Find the geometry chart for your own bike (search its make, model and "geometry" — or measure the seat tube and chainstays yourself with a tape measure). Write down its six numbers next to the Trucker's and note the two biggest differences. Bring them to our next chat — they'll tell us a lot about what your bike is designed to do, and it's your first act of chart criticism.
