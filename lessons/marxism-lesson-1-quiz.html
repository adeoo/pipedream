Question 1 · Day 1 — "Bread before philosophy"

Why have evangelical churches multiplied fastest in São Paulo's poorest neighbourhoods? Which answer is the materialist one?

Something to chew on Your lesson's closing exercise, if you skipped it: pick one belief common in your city and explain it by pointing at a material condition instead of another belief. Harder than it sounds!

Question 2 · Day 1 — the famous quote

"It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness." Does this mean people are puppets of economics?

Something to chew on The lesson's political edge: if poverty came from bad ideas, preaching would end it. If it comes from how production and ownership are arranged, persuasion alone never will. Do you buy that distinction between charity and politics?

Question 3 · Day 2 — "A photograph of a fistfight"

June 2013: bus fares in São Paulo rise twenty centavos, and within two weeks a million people are in the streets. What does this illustrate, dialectically?

Something to chew on Your lesson's question back to you: where in your own working life has pressure been quietly accumulating — and what might the threshold look like?

Question 4 · Day 2 — internal contradiction

In dialectics, what is a "contradiction" inside an arrangement?

Hint from your email: think of the employer who needs to pay workers as little as possible… and needs customers with money to spend.

Something to chew on The Day 2 exercise: name one contradiction in your own workplace — two things the arrangement needs at the same time that work against each other. Can you find one?

Question 5 · Day 3 — "What a salary cannot tell you"

A senior nurse and a lanchonete owner both earn R$6,000 a month. For Marx, what separates them?

Something to chew on The Day 3 exercise, if you dodged it: place yourself in one sentence — what do you sell, or what do you own, in order to live, and to whom?

Question 6 · Day 3 — the app driver

An app driver owns his own motorcycle and is told he's an entrepreneur. What did your lesson say about that?

Something to chew on The lesson admits grey zones honestly: managers paid to command others' labour, professionals half-selling skill and half-owning a practice. Who's the most borderline person you know? Where would you place them, and why?

Pause & think · No wrong answers

"There is no money for hospitals."

Day 4's exercise: take one sentence you hear as pure common sense — at work or in the news — and name who benefits if everyone keeps believing it. Use the hospital one, or pick your own.

The lesson's angle: ideology is strongest where it's least visible — in what goes without saying. "No money for hospitals" is said in the same year debt is serviced without discussion. The trick isn't lying; it's the horizon: some questions asked endlessly (do wages cause inflation?), others never (do profits?). Whatever sentence you chose, the follow-up question is always the same: who set this horizon, and what sits outside it?

Question 7 · Day 4 — "Whose common sense"

"The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas." How does this actually work, according to your lesson?

Something to chew on Your lesson's opening: a metro strike is "the city held hostage," but years of wages eaten by inflation is… not news at all. Can you catch the vocabulary taking a side in today's headlines?

Question 8 · Day 5 — "Printed in February, tested in June"

Why did all of Europe explode in one season in 1848? The materialist answer starts…

Something to chew on Notice how Day 5 quietly used Day 1's method on history itself. Does the materialist explanation of 1848 convince you more than "the revolution spread because the ideas were powerful"? What would count as evidence either way?

Question 9 · Day 5 — the June days

In June 1848 the broad revolutionary alliance broke apart in Paris. What was the lesson "written in blood" that Marx drew from it?

Something to chew on Today's closing exercise from your email: name a modern alliance you've seen break the moment property was threatened. And the question the course promises to keep returning to — when bread got cheaper and order returned, which class had kept its gains?

Pause & think · Your turn to push back

What's your strongest doubt after five days?

Good readers of Marx — including his sharpest critics — engage rather than memorize. What's one claim from this week you're not convinced by, or that doesn't fit the world you see? Write it down.

Why this matters: whatever you wrote is your best study guide. Your own course keeps its claims honest — Day 1 warned against the puppet version of materialism, Day 3 admitted the grey zones, Day 4 said ruling ideas don't reign unopposed. Chasing your doubt through the coming lessons (Lebanon arrives tomorrow!) will teach you more than agreeing with everything ever could. Keep it; revisit it after the Day 7 review.
