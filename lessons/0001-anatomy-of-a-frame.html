Bike Frame Craft · Lesson 1

The Anatomy of a Frame

Before you can design a frame, you need to speak its language. Today you learn the eight parts of the classic diamond frame — and why that shape has survived 130 years almost unchanged.

Why every frame is two triangles

Look at almost any bike — including the touring bike you'll one day design — and you'll see the same skeleton: the diamond frame. It's really two triangles sharing an edge. Triangles are the strongest simple shape in engineering: push on a corner, and the sides can only stretch or squash — they can't fold. That's why bridges are built from triangles, and why your frame can weigh under 2 kg yet carry you, your groceries, and a month of touring luggage.

The grand tour: eight parts

The front triangle (bronze above) is made of four members — we'll get to that little scandal in a moment:

  • Head tube — the short stub at the front. The fork's steerer spins inside it; it literally holds your steering (Sheldon Brown).
  • Top tube — connects head tube to seat tube. The one you swing your leg over.
  • Down tube — head tube down to the bottom bracket. The biggest, hardest-working tube in the frame.
  • Seat tube — bottom bracket up to the saddle clamp. Your seatpost slides into it.

The rear triangle (green) is the seat tube plus two pairs of skinny tubes, one on each side of the rear wheel:

  • Chain stays — from the bottom bracket straight back to the rear wheel. Every watt you pedal travels through these.
  • Seat stays — from the top of the seat tube down to the rear wheel, completing the triangle (Sheldon Brown).

Two more small parts complete the vocabulary:

  • Bottom bracket shell — the stubby cylinder where seat tube, down tube and chain stays all meet. The crank axle spins inside it. It's the heart of the frame: nearly every design measurement starts here.
  • Dropouts — the small slotted plates at the very ends of the stays where the rear wheel bolts in.

The scandal: the "front triangle" has four sides — head tube, top tube, down tube, seat tube. Framebuilders call it a triangle anyway, because the short head tube barely counts. Knowing this earns you a knowing nod on any framebuilding forum.

Why this matters for your touring frame

Per your mission, everything we learn points at a city/touring frame. These eight parts are where all your future design decisions live: how long the chain stays are decides whether panniers hit your heels; the head tube's angle decides how the bike steers with a load; the seat tube's angle and length decide how the bike fits you. Learn the names now, and every geometry chart you meet in Lesson 2 will read like a sentence instead of a spreadsheet.

Your real-world assignment

Go to your own bike. Touch each of the eight parts and say its name out loud, without looking anything up. It takes ninety seconds and it moves these names from "just read it" to "know it" — spoken retrieval beats rereading every time. Tomorrow, do it once more.
